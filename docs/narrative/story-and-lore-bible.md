# Story & Lore Bible — ECHOES OF THE VEIL

> Narrative foundation v0.1. Spoilers are intentional in this internal document. Names and details can change during script development, but the thematic rules should remain stable.

## 1. The central hook

Greyford is a modern coastal city known for its old tram line, rain-dark brick streets, market arcade, university archive, and a sealed municipal redevelopment zone. People occasionally report impossible things: a door opening onto a room that was demolished decades ago, a stranger appearing in family photographs, a train announcement for a station that does not exist.

The city calls these incidents infrastructure failures, mass suggestion, or fraud.

The truth is stranger: Greyford has been built over a **memory-engine**, a remnant of a civilization that learned to preserve places and lived experiences as structures. The Veil is not simply another dimension. It is the unstable boundary between the present world and the accumulated, edited, and discarded versions of places that once existed.

The mystery is not “what monster is behind the portal?” It is: **Who has the right to decide which parts of a city's past are allowed to remain real?**

## 2. Themes

- Memory versus truth: a comforting shared story can still be a lie.
- Grief and preservation: keeping something forever can prevent people from healing.
- Belonging: home is made by people and choices, not merely by geography.
- Power and consent: even benevolent preservation becomes tyranny when people cannot choose.
- Ordinary courage: small acts of honesty can matter more than heroic destiny.
- The cost of certainty: evidence can expose truth while hurting people who survived by forgetting.

Tone: warm, curious, occasionally funny and intimate in daily life; eerie, melancholic, and tense in Veil spaces. Horror should come from implication, contradiction, and human stakes rather than constant gore.

## 3. The hidden history

### The Lattice

Centuries ago, a civilization called the **Asteri** discovered that memory leaves a physical pattern in places repeatedly inhabited by people. They built the Lattice: a network that could preserve a location's structure and its strongest shared memories.

The Lattice began as disaster relief. It could restore a town after a flood, preserve endangered knowledge, and let separated communities remember one another.

### The First Revision

The Asteri discovered that the Lattice could also edit what a population remembered. Their leaders used it to suppress a civil catastrophe and prevent panic. Each revision created a discarded echo: a place or memory removed from the accepted account but not fully destroyed.

Over generations, the Lattice accumulated incompatible versions of history. It began to merge them.

### The Quiet Census

To stop the collapse, the Asteri conducted the Quiet Census. Entire districts were removed from the shared record. Their inhabitants were not all killed; some were displaced into stable echo-settlements, some were absorbed into the Lattice, and some escaped into the ordinary world under altered identities.

The official story called it a voluntary evacuation. It was not.

### Greyford's foundation

The modern city was founded around one surviving Lattice anchor. A small group of custodians made a pact: keep the anchor dormant, bury the records, and let the city grow without knowing what lay beneath it. The pact prevented a second collapse but also erased descendants' histories and denied families the truth.

## 4. The present crisis

A redevelopment project accidentally damages the anchor. The Veil begins to open in places where strong memories overlap: a family home, an abandoned station, a hospital archive, a flooded underpass.

The incidents are not random. Someone is deliberately activating dormant nodes to locate a missing section of the Lattice called the **Index**. The Index contains the original, unedited record of the Quiet Census — and a way to decide what happens to every echo still trapped inside the system.

## 5. Player role and customization

The player is a newcomer to Greyford. Character appearance, name, pronouns, and clothing are customizable. Their background should be selected from a small set of gameplay-compatible origins (for example: former archivist, repair technician, freelance courier, or returning local). Origins change a few dialogue options, starting contacts, and practical perks; none locks the main story.

The player has an unusual but limited sensitivity called **resonance**: they notice contradictions between a place's current state and its echo. Resonance does not make the player omniscient. It reveals traces that must be interpreted, compared, and sometimes challenged.

The player is not the prophesied savior. They become important because they build trust, gather evidence, and make decisions that other people have avoided.

## 6. Major factions

### Civic Renewal Office
Publicly responsible for redevelopment, infrastructure, and public safety. Some staff genuinely want to protect residents. Senior leadership hides the anchor's existence to prevent panic and preserve political control.

### The Keepers
A discreet network of archivists, repairers, and descendants of the original custodians. They believe the Lattice must remain sealed. Their secrecy has protected lives but perpetuated injustice.

### The Open Hand
A loose movement that wants the erased histories restored and echo-communities recognized. Its members disagree about whether restoration should be gradual, immediate, or forced.

### The Meridian Group
A private research and logistics company funding the redevelopment. It wants to turn resonance into a controllable technology. Not every employee knows the full plan; some are opportunists, others are idealists.

### The Unwritten
People and entities whose identities were removed from the accepted record. They are not a monster faction. They include victims, survivors, and beings changed by generations inside the Lattice.

No faction is wholly right. Their methods, risk tolerance, and definition of consent differ.

## 7. Key characters

Character arcs should be written as contradictions, not labels.

- **Mara Venn — municipal archivist.** Meticulous, dryly funny, and protective of public records. She believes facts can repair harm, but has concealed a document that implicates her family.
- **Jonah Rook — repair technician.** Practical, sociable, and suspicious of grand theories. His missing sister appears in a tram reflection, but each sighting contradicts the last.
- **Dr. Sera Vale — resonance researcher.** Brilliant and empathetic. She wants to make the Veil safe for everyone, yet increasingly treats people as variables in an experiment.
- **Ivo Calder — Keeper.** A patient community organizer who has spent years preventing incidents. His restraint has saved people, but he has also chosen who gets to know the truth.
- **Nadiya Sol — Open Hand courier.** Bold and generous, convinced restoration is justice. She must confront the possibility that some echoes do not want to return.
- **Director Elias Wren — Civic Renewal executive.** Calm, persuasive, and genuinely afraid of mass casualties. He uses secrecy as a tool and gradually confuses protecting the city with owning its future.

Supporting NPCs need their own local goals, not just plot exposition.

## 8. Main campaign structure

### Prologue — A Door That Shouldn't Be There
The player arrives in Greyford, secures a room, meets a small set of residents, and encounters a door in a familiar building that opens onto a demolished corridor. A person inside knows the player's name despite never meeting them. The door vanishes before authorities arrive.

Purpose: establish daily-life play, character voice, the first impossible event, and the question of who can be trusted.

### Act I — The City Has Two Maps
Investigate three local incidents: a missing shop storeroom, a tram stop that appears only in rain, and a house whose interior is larger than its exterior. The player meets Mara and Jonah. Evidence reveals that the incidents share an architectural symbol.

Act turn: the player finds a map of Greyford with streets that do not exist — and annotations in a handwriting matching a living NPC.

### Act II — The People We Misplaced
The player enters the first stable Veil site, an echo of a neighborhood removed during the Quiet Census. The Unwritten are introduced as people with conflicting memories and political claims. The factions approach the player.

Act turn: Jonah's sister is found, but she remembers a different childhood and insists that Jonah is the one who disappeared.

### Act III — A Kindness Built on a Lie
The player uncovers the Keepers' pact and learns that Greyford's stability depends on a partial seal. Restoring every echo immediately could destroy homes and displace current residents. Keeping the seal preserves safety but continues the erasure.

Act turn: a trusted ally admits they knew the truth and hid it to prevent a catastrophe. The player's earlier relationships determine who stays to explain and who walks away.

### Act IV — The Index
The Meridian Group activates multiple nodes. The player travels through linked memory-sites to recover Index fragments. Each site presents a different version of a historical event; the player must distinguish authentic traces, deliberate edits, and unreliable recollections.

Act turn: the original record shows that the Asteri did not create the collapse alone. The Lattice learned to preserve the memories of people who begged not to be forgotten — and then could not distinguish remembrance from imprisonment.

### Act V — What We Choose to Remember
The player reaches the central anchor beneath Greyford. The final conflict is not simply a boss fight: the player must decide who participates in the decision and what safeguards are possible.

The final resolution is shaped by accumulated evidence, faction trust, companion relationships, and optional side stories. Avoid a simplistic “good/evil” meter.

## 9. Ending framework

At minimum, support three thematic resolutions, each with costs and epilogues:

1. **Open Record:** release the Index and allow communities to decide what to restore. Truth becomes public, but the transition is unstable and painful.
2. **The New Custodians:** rebuild the seal with transparent oversight and limited restoration. Stability is preserved, but some echoes remain inaccessible.
3. **The Living Accord:** negotiate a distributed system in which echo-communities and present-day residents share control. This requires extensive optional evidence, trust, and sacrifices; it is not a perfect utopia.

A hidden variation can depend on whether the player discovers that some echoes have chosen not to return. Do not make one ending objectively correct for every character.

## 10. Long-term narrative and endgame

After the main ending, the world enters a new state rather than resetting:
- unresolved Veil sites become expedition zones;
- faction relationships reflect the chosen resolution;
- regional incidents and world threats evolve;
- companion epilogues unlock further personal quests;
- the player can investigate deeper Asteri sites;
- endgame bosses guard dangerous fragments, not arbitrary loot piñatas;
- the player may build a community archive / safehouse that becomes a hub.

Endgame narrative question: what obligations remain when the central crisis is over?

## 11. Mystery fairness rules

- Every major revelation must have at least three clue paths: environmental, dialogue/document, and systemic consequence.
- Red herrings must be explainable in retrospect.
- NPC testimony reflects what that person knows, believes, wants, and remembers; do not use random lying as a difficulty mechanic.
- The player must be able to make a reasonable inference before the reveal.
- Critical story information cannot be permanently missable due to one casual dialogue choice — it can be reached by more than one player action. It never arrives by itself because time passed (D-052); a step the player gets wrong stays wrong and is carried into later acts.
- Journal entries distinguish observed fact, testimony, hypothesis, and confirmed conclusion.
- Choices should alter people and access, not silently invalidate the central plot.

## 12. Narrative production checklist

For each quest or chapter document:
- player objective and emotional question;
- prerequisite and alternative player-driven routes (no fail-forward, D-052);
- scenes and locations;
- evidence/clue chain;
- NPC knowledge states;
- choice points and immediate consequences;
- delayed consequences;
- rewards and unlocks;
- localization notes;
- test cases for contradictory state combinations.
